# 1959 — narration script

Read by an AI voice (piper, en_US-amy-medium). One WAV per beat, so each beat's timing can be
nudged on its own. Words are the poster text, lightly adapted for the ear.

Voice direction: calm, plain, unhurried. The tape does the drama.

---

## Beat 1 — cold open (0:00–0:08)

No words. Black screen, red "1959", tape clicks.

## Beat 2 — the experiment (0:08–0:40)

In 1959, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel lowered an electrode into the
visual cortex of a cat, and moved a bar of light across its field of view.
A single neuron answered, and its spikes were audible as clicks on tape.
The clicks won them the 1981 Nobel Prize. They meant: seeing is built,
cell by cell, from single features of the world.

## Beat 3 — the chip (0:40–1:10)

This piece plays that recording into a chip designed by Federico Corradi:
sixteen analog spiking neurons on one piece of silicon, three point six by
five point two millimetres. Each neuron is a circuit, like this one: a
capacitor holds the membrane voltage, a transistor leaks it away, and a
comparator watches for the threshold. It has no clock and computes no numbers.
It answers in continuous time, in physics. And it never answers identically.
No two neurons, born or made, ever do.

## Beat 4 — the synapse (1:10–1:25)

Every click on the tape becomes one spike, delivered to neuron fourteen
through one synapse. This is its circuit: a few transistors that turn a
digital spike into a pulse of current. The weight is electric charge — how
much current, for how long. Not a number stored in memory: a physical fact.

## Beat 5 — the array (1:25–1:40)

Here is the whole array on the die: sixteen neurons, and the synapses that
wire them together. Sixteen neurons is not a brain. It is the start of a
different bet: that machines which have to perceive, in real time, on the
power the world can spare, should be made of physics rather than numbers.

## Beat 6 — the piece (1:40–2:00)

The visitor hears both: a cat's cortex from 1959, and a silicon neuron
from 2026, in the same room, sixty-seven years apart — almost in unison.
Those clicks are where machine vision began. Today's neural networks are
a diagram of this cat.

## Beat 7 — coda (2:00–2:10)

No words. Card, red rule, clicks fade:

> What they copied was the wiring. What they left behind was the body.
